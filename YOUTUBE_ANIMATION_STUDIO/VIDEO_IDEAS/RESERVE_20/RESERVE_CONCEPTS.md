### SCUBA-R1 — They Drifted to Dragon Island
*Alt title:* Lost at Sea, Then a Komodo Dragon · *Score:* **80/100** (D15 S17 P17 R16 F15) · *Difficulty:* Medium · *Duration:* 58 s

**Subject:** June 2008, Komodo National Park, Indonesia: five divers (three British, one French, one Swedish) were swept away by strong currents off Tatawa island. They drifted for more than 12 hours, about 20 miles, and washed ashore on remote Rinca island, where a Komodo dragon repeatedly approached. They drove it off with rocks and driftwood. A search with ~30 boats found them the next day; they were dehydrated but had no serious injuries. Reported ordeal length varies (~36 h to 2 days; NBC's 'five days' is an outlier).

**Why it can work:** A double-threat survival story: open-water drift (like the Lonergans, but they lived) then a prehistoric predator. The twist lands mid-video and the outcome is positive. It is a natural Short with a loopable first frame (heads in the sea, then a dragon on the beach).

**Verification:** VERIFIED (CBS, NBC, Jakarta Post, Dawn, VOA). Ordeal duration varies (36 h to 2 days) - say 'more than a day'. Do not invent names or dialogue.

**15-second hook:**

- 0-2 s: Five tiny heads in a swirling current, the dive boat gone. VO: 'Five divers surfaced and their boat was gone.'
- 2-6 s: 'Komodo National Park, 2008'. Drift-line map.
- 6-15 s: VO: 'They drifted all night to an island. Then something came out of the trees.' A dragon silhouette on the sand.

**Structure:**

1. Hook: empty sea
2. Komodo's notorious currents (map arrows)
3. Drift through the night; they tie together (reported)
4. Landfall on Rinca island
5. Twist: Komodo dragon approaches; rocks and driftwood
6. Search: ~30 boats; spotted waving on shore
7. Outcome: all five survive
8. Loop: 'the sea nearly took them, and the island nearly finished the job'

**Retention moments:** 0 s empty sea; 10 s current arrows; 25 s night drift; 32 s dragon reveal; 45 s rescue boat; 56 s loop

**Thumbnail / first frame:** Five tiny divers on a moonlit beach, huge Komodo dragon silhouette in the foreground, 'DRAGON ISLAND'.

**Animation style:** Open-ocean current shader with flow arrows, night sky, low-poly Komodo dragon (simple quadruped rig), rocky beach; no injury depiction.

**Scenes:** Dive boat and current map; Divers floating at dusk; Night drift with stars; Rinca island beach; Dragon approach; Rescue boat at dawn

### SCUBA-R2 — She Let Sharks Bite Her on Purpose
*Alt title:* The Chain-Mail Shark Test · *Score:* **74/100** (D14 S14 P17 R14 F15) · *Difficulty:* Low · *Duration:* 52 s

**Subject:** Australian diver and filmmaker Valerie Taylor tested a stainless-steel chain-mail (mesh) suit, conceived by her husband Ron Taylor, by letting sharks bite her arm. Experiments ran from around 1980 (grey nurse sharks, then blue sharks off California). A National Geographic cover showing a blue shark biting her mail-clad arm is dated 1981 by Wikipedia and 1982 by CNN and others. Documentary 'Playing with Sharks' (2021).

**Why it can work:** An absurd, visually iconic premise ('let a shark bite you') with a scientific payoff (the suit spreads bite force) and an underdog pioneer. A strong first frame. The story has no deaths, so it suits a wide audience.

**Verification:** VERIFIED (Wikipedia, CNN, Australian National Maritime Museum collection, Australian Museum). Cover year 1981 vs 1982 differs - say 'early 1980s'. No National Geographic logo on screen.

**15-second hook:**

- 0-2 s: A shark's jaws clamp on a silver mesh arm. VO: 'She let this shark bite her, on purpose.'
- 2-6 s: 'Australia / California, early 1980s'.
- 6-15 s: VO: 'The suit was made for her husband. It was too small for him, so she tested it herself.'

**Structure:**

1. Hook bite
2. Who the Taylors were (filmmakers, ex-spearfishers)
3. Ron's chain-mail idea; too small for him
4. Tests: grey nurse sharks, then blue sharks
5. Mechanism: thousands of rings spread the bite (no puncture)
6. Magazine cover moment (1981/82)
7. Legacy: conservationist; 'Playing with Sharks' (2021)
8. Loop

**Retention moments:** 0 s bite; 8 s 'too small for him'; 28 s ring-mesh mechanism zoom; 40 s cover-style freeze frame; 50 s loop

### SCUBA-R3 — He Thought It Was a Dead Body
*Alt title:* The Arm in the Sand: Riace Bronzes · *Score:* **73/100** (D13 S15 P16 R13 F16) · *Difficulty:* Low · *Duration:* 50 s

**Subject:** On 16 Aug 1972, Stefano Mariottini, a chemist from Rome on holiday, was diving about 200 m off Riace Marina, Calabria, Italy, when he saw an arm emerging from the sand. He reportedly feared it was a corpse. It was one of two 5th-century BC Greek bronze warriors (Riace bronzes), now in the National Archaeological Museum of Magna Graecia, Reggio Calabria. Details are disputed: he has described spearfishing on breath-hold, other accounts say snorkelling; the seabed depth is ~6 m (his 2009 account) or ~10 m (1972 report); questions about possible missing items remain (Le Iene, 2019).

**Why it can work:** A macabre-to-wonder twist (corpse becomes a masterpiece) in the first 10 s. A lone-holidaymaker discovery and an unresolved 'what else was down there' coda. Statues are simple to model.

**Verification:** VERIFIED (Wikipedia, World History Encyclopedia, Atlas Obscura, La Nación). DISPUTED details (snorkel vs spearfishing, 6 vs 10 m, missing finds) labelled. 'Silver teeth / copper lips' not checked this pass - drop or verify before scripting. Note: not scuba - he was on breath-hold.

**15-second hook:**

- 0-2 s: In sandy shallows, a dark human arm reaches up out of the seabed. VO: 'In 1972 a holidaymaker saw an arm sticking out of the sand.'
- 2-6 s: 'Riace, Italy'. He freezes.
- 6-15 s: VO: 'He thought he'd found a body. He touched it. It was bronze, and 2,400 years old.'

**Structure:**

1. Hook arm
2. The swimmer (chemist on holiday)
3. Touch: metal, not flesh
4. Recovery of two life-size warriors
5. Restoration reveals detail (silver teeth, copper lips - reported)
6. Mystery: depth discrepancies; missing items questioned (labelled)
7. Loop: 'he still won't say exactly what he saw'

**Retention moments:** 0 s arm reveal; 10 s 'bronze' twist; 25 s two statues rise; 38 s DISPUTED label; 48 s loop

### SCUBA-R4 — 27,831 Dives to Raise Henry VIII's Warship
*Alt title:* The Tudor Ship That Came Back Up · *Score:* **69/100** (D13 S14 P14 R13 F15) · *Difficulty:* Medium · *Duration:* 60 s

**Subject:** The Mary Rose, Henry VIII's warship, sank in the Solent in 1545. Alexander McKee's 'Project Solent Ships' (with Southsea British Sub-Aqua Club divers, from 1965) located the wreck; diver Percy Ackland found timbers in May 1971. A volunteer-heavy excavation made 27,831 dives, and the hull was raised on 11 Oct 1982. It is now displayed at the Mary Rose Museum, Portsmouth.

**Why it can work:** An epic number hook (27,831 dives), an amateur-divers-beat-the-odds arc and a tense live lift (the lifting frame famously slipped, reported). Steady evergreen history demand, but slower than survival stories.

**Verification:** VERIFIED (Mary Rose Trust x2, World History Encyclopedia, History Hit). 27,831 dives and 11 Oct 1982 confirmed. Discovery date 1 vs 5 May 1971 - say 'May 1971'. Lifting-frame slip and 'sank in front of the king' are reported - check before scripting.

- 2-6 s: Tudor warship silhouette heeling over. '1545'.
- 6-15 s: VO: 'It had been lost for over 400 years, and amateur divers found it.'

**Structure:**

1. Hook dive counter
2. 1545: sinks in front of the king (reported)
3. 1965: McKee starts the search
4. 1971: first timbers found
5. Excavation in near-zero visibility; 27,831 dives
6. 11 Oct 1982: the lift
7. Today: museum hall
8. Loop

**Retention moments:** 0 s counter; 12 s ship heels over; 30 s first timber; 45 s lift tension; 58 s loop

### SCUBA-R5 — They Lived on the Seafloor for a Month
*Alt title:* Cousteau's Underwater Village · *Score:* **68/100** (D12 S13 P15 R12 F16) · *Difficulty:* Medium · *Duration:* 55 s

**Subject:** Conshelf II (Précontinent II), 1963: Jacques Cousteau's team built an underwater village at Sha'ab Rumi reef, ~25 miles NE of Port Sudan in the Red Sea. A starfish-shaped house at ~10 m sheltered up to six aquanauts for about 30 days. It also had a hangar for the Diving Saucer and a deeper cabin (~27 m). The film 'World Without Sun' won an Academy Award. The hangar structure remains on the reef today.

**Why it can work:** A wonder/sci-fi premise (people lived underwater) with strong visuals that suit procedural building. A 'you can still dive it today' payoff. Low stakes mean a lower retention score, but it is a good tonal palate-cleanser.

**Verification:** VERIFIED (National Geographic, TIME, Cousteau Society, Smithsonian Ocean). Duration '~30 days' (one source: three weeks). Depths 10 m and ~27 m. The Starfish house was removed and the hangar remains (Bradt, single source - mark 'reported').

**15-second hook:**

- 0-2 s: A glowing starfish-shaped house on a reef at night, fish at the windows. VO: 'In 1963, people lived on the seafloor for a month.'
- 2-6 s: 'Red Sea, off Sudan'.
- 6-15 s: VO: 'They slept, ate and worked 10 metres down, and part of their village is still there.'

**Structure:**

1. Hook habitat
2. Why: could humans colonise the continental shelf?
3. Layout: Starfish house, saucer hangar, deep cabin
4. Daily life (meals, sleep, work dives) - from documented accounts only
5. Risks: pressure, saturation
6. Oscar-winning film
7. Today: hangar remains, coral-covered
8. Loop

**Retention moments:** 0 s glowing habitat; 12 s cutaway layout; 30 s deep cabin descent; 45 s coral-covered 'today'; 54 s loop

**Thumbnail / first frame:** Starfish-shaped habitat glowing on a reef at night, tiny diver outside, 'THEY LIVED HERE'.
